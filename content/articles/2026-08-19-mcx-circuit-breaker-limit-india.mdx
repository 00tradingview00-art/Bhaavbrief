## Introduction

Understanding how the MCX circuit breaker limit works in India is essential for any trader or merchant holding open positions in gold, silver, crude oil, or base metals. When a commodity contract moves beyond a pre-set percentage threshold within a single session, MCX activates a price band mechanism that temporarily halts or restricts further trading.

## The Mechanism

MCX operates under a tiered circuit breaker system mandated by SEBI. Each commodity contract carries a predefined daily price limit — typically expressed as a percentage of the previous day's settlement price. For most metals, this sits at ±3% to ±6%, while crude oil contracts can carry limits up to ±5%.

The transmission works as follows:

1. **Trigger**: The contract's last traded price (LTP) touches the upper or lower circuit limit.
2. **First halt**: Trading pauses for 15 minutes. MCX resets the band by expanding it — usually by an additional 1–3 percentage points.
3. **Second breach**: If price again hits the expanded limit, a second 15-minute halt is called.
4. **Final cap**: If the expanded band is breached a third time, trading in that contract stops for the remainder of the session at the final circuit level. No further orders execute beyond that price.

The circuit price itself is calculated as:

> **Circuit Price = Previous Day Settlement Price × (1 ± Circuit Limit %)**

For agri-commodities under SEBI's special provisions, the percentage bands are tighter and the expansion steps are smaller, reflecting their sensitivity to domestic food inflation policy.

## India-Specific Context

India's MCX prices do not mirror global benchmarks in real time — several structural layers sit between COMEX gold or NYMEX crude and what an Indian trader sees on screen.

The import parity price for gold, for instance, is calculated as:

> **MCX Gold Price ≈ COMEX Price × USD/INR Rate × (1 + Import Duty %) × (1 + GST %)**

Currently, gold carries a 15% import duty and 3% GST, meaning a 1% move in COMEX or a 1% rupee depreciation creates an amplified INR price move. For crude oil, the MCX contract references NYMEX WTI, converted at the prevailing USD/INR rate, with no import duty buffer but significant state-level cess affecting downstream sentiment. SEBI's commodity derivatives regulations also prohibit certain participants — including banks — from freely hedging on MCX, which can reduce liquidity precisely when circuits are most likely to fire.

## Historical Episodes

In March 2020, MCX crude oil futures hit lower circuits repeatedly as global demand collapsed during COVID-19 lockdowns. The April 2020 WTI negative-price event caused MCX crude to fall approximately 30–35% within days, triggering circuit halts across multiple sessions before MCX revised its settlement framework.

In 2022, following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, MCX natural gas futures surged over 20% in a compressed two-week window, hitting upper circuits on several consecutive days as European supply anxiety fed through USD/INR conversion.

In 2023, gold on MCX breached its upper circuit intraday on at least two occasions when the rupee depreciated sharply alongside a spike in COMEX prices, with single-session moves exceeding ₹1,500 per 10 grams.

## What to Watch

These specific data points signal that the MCX circuit breaker limit mechanism is under stress:

- **USD/INR spot rate** — sharp intraday moves of ₹0.50 or more amplify import parity prices immediately
- **RBI MPC meeting dates** — rate decisions shift rupee volatility windows
- **NYMEX/COMEX settlement times** — MCX prices gap at opening when overnight moves are large
- **EIA weekly inventory report** (every Wednesday, 8:00 PM IST) — primary crude circuit trigger
- **SEBI's commodity circuit limit circulars** — periodically revised; check before major macro events
- **MCX daily margin report** — rising margins often precede circuit-prone sessions
